The Tape Speed Audit

Perera's volume-profile mean-reversion paper reports a 354% return and a Sharpe of 3.98. Rebuilt from its own rules and run on four years of real MNQ data, it loses money in every one of 238 configurations tested.

Verdict: do not trade this

The paper's headline result is an artifact of a look-ahead bias it discloses in its own limitations section. Its profit target is the current day's Point of Control — a level that cannot be known until the day is over. Remove that single piece of hindsight and the strategy's profit factor falls from 3.06 to 0.93.

Of 238 honest configurations swept across stops, targets, thresholds, sessions and profile methods, 16 made money, none reached statistical significance, and every single one breached your $2,000 trailing drawdown — the median configuration drew down $29,581, roughly fifteen times your limit.

There is a faint real tendency buried in the entry signal, and section 3 measures it. It is worth roughly $2.70 per trade against ±90 points of noise — about one-tenth of what the $3,000 monthly target requires, and pointed in a direction the paper's exit design systematically throws away.

01

The result is made of hindsight

The paper's strategy fades the previous day's value-area edges back toward the Point of Control, with a momentum filter it calls Tape Speed. The rules are mechanical enough to implement exactly, so I did — twice. The two runs are identical in every respect but one: what the profit target is allowed to know.

As the paper computes it

$249,209

Target = the session's final POC, computed from all of the day's volume — including bars that had not printed when the trade was opened. Win rate 73.0%, profit factor 3.06.

With the future removed

$37,666

Target = the developing POC, built only from bars already closed at the moment of entry. Same signals, same stops, same costs. Win rate 48.9%, profit factor 0.93.

The paper says so itself. §5.4, Limitation 4: "The current-day POC is computed using full-day data, creating a subtle look-ahead bias. In live trading, the developing POC would shift intraday." It is not subtle, and it is not a limitation — it is the finding.

Two further reasons the study proves nothing

  • The price data is invented. §3.1: "this study employs a synthetic data generation approach." The generator is specified as log-price reversion to equilibrium with a ~30-day half-life — mean reversion was written into the data by hand, and the paper then reports that a mean-reversion strategy profits from it. That conclusion was guaranteed before any trade was simulated.
  • The Monte Carlo is circular. It resamples the same synthetic trades. Bootstrapping a distribution whose mean is positive and reporting a 100% probability of profit is arithmetic, not evidence.
02

No configuration rescues it

A single failing parameter set proves little, so I swept the space: stop distances from 15 to 250 points plus ATR- and percentage-based stops, Tape Speed thresholds from 0.0 to 1.2, both session definitions, both value-area algorithms, four profile bin widths, one and three trades per day, and four exit designs. 238 runs in total, every one of them free of look-ahead.

The best configuration of all 238 earned $15.35 per trade at a t-statistic of 1.33. That is the maximum of 238 attempts: picking the best of 238 noisy results will hand you something that looks mildly positive even when nothing is there, and a t-statistic of 1.33 is exactly what "nothing is there" looks like. The distribution of t-statistics across the whole sweep centres on −1.24.

The median configuration's worst drawdown was $29,581. Your account fails at $2,000. There is no position size that fixes this, because the drawdown scales with contracts exactly as the profit does.
03

What is actually in the signal

Rather than judge the strategy only through its own stop-and-target machinery, I measured the raw premise directly: after a valid entry signal, which way does price actually go? No stops, no targets, no costs — just the forward move in the trade's direction, across 81,065 signals and 946 sessions, with errors clustered by session so that overlapping windows are not counted as independent.

The positive mean comes entirely from a fat right tail — rare, large favourable runs.

This is the useful finding. The signal behaves like a momentum signal, not a mean-reversion one — which makes sense, since Tape Speed requires momentum in the trade's direction before entering. Its edge lives in rare large winners. The paper's exit design does precisely the wrong thing with that shape: a nearby POC target caps the large winners, while a fixed stop realises the frequent small adverse moves. The exit is fighting the signal.

Testing that directly: replacing the POC target with an 8R target or a trailing stop, changing nothing else, flips the strategy from −$12,334 to +$13,982. It confirms the diagnosis. It does not produce a tradeable system — as the next section shows.

04

The profit is one regime, not an edge

A strategy with a genuine edge earns in most years. Both the paper's version and the improved-exit version earn in one.

Out-of-sample testing confirms this is regime dependence rather than a fitted result. Splitting at January 2025, every candidate configuration lost in 2022–24 and won in 2025–26. That pattern is the opposite of overfitting, and it is not reassuring: it means the strategy's viability depends on a market character that persisted for about eighteen months. Nothing in the data tells you whether it is still present, and a prop evaluation gives you one month to find out.

05

Against your account rules

The final question is the practical one: run on your $50,000 account with a $2,000 trailing drawdown, what happens in a one-month evaluation? I bootstrapped 5,000 month-long sequences from the best variant's own trades — giving it the benefit of its favourable regime — and applied the trailing limit trade by trade, including the intraday adverse excursion that trails the limit upward on unrealised profit.

At your intended 5–6 micros the strategy passes roughly one month in four and destroys the account in roughly three. Even the least-bad ratio, at 3 micros, is 6.9% pass against 14.8% blow-up — two to one against you.

The arithmetic of the $3,000 target

Using the best variant's full-period result, including its good regime, the strategy nets $55.93 per micro per month. Reaching $3,000 a month would require 54 micros — just under your 60-contract cap, and carrying a drawdown roughly eleven times your $2,000 limit. The requirement and the constraint are not close to compatible.

Worth noting for any future strategy. Your target implies about $143 of expectancy per trade at one trade per day with 5 micros. That is roughly 14 points of net edge per trade on MNQ. For calibration, the entire measured drift in this signal over a full two hours is 2.7 points. A strategy that clears your bar needs to be an order of magnitude better than this one — not a tuned version of it.
06

Where I would look next

Two things in this analysis are worth carrying forward, and one habit is worth adopting.

  1. Your own method fits the data better than this paper does. The measured signal pays through rare large winners, not frequent small ones. Your described approach — high reward-to-risk, small losses, setups every ten to fourteen days — is shaped correctly for that. This paper's design is not. If you want a bot built, your own model is the better candidate, and I can specify and test it the same way.
  2. The consistency rule still needs solving. At two or three trades a month, one trade is necessarily more than 40% of the month's profit, so you can fail consistency while making money. That constraint should be designed for up front, not discovered during an evaluation.
  3. Check the data source before the logic. This paper was readable, well-formatted, properly referenced and entirely hollow, and the single sentence that gave it away was in §3.1. When you send me the next study, the first thing I will check is whether its results come from a market or from a random number generator.

What the backtest assumed

So you can judge the numbers yourself: entries fill at the next bar's open after a closed-bar signal, with one tick of slippage; stops are market orders paying two further ticks; targets are resting limits requiring genuine trade-through; when a bar contains both stop and target the stop is assumed to fill first; commissions are $1.20 round turn per micro. Position sizing is fixed, no overnight holds, and profiles are rebuilt each session with the roll flagged so a splice never contaminates the previous day's levels. All of these choices are conservative but not extreme — loosening them does not change the conclusion, because the shortfall is roughly tenfold, not marginal.
